"We have free public healthcare and I have ACC. Why would I pay for health insurance on top of that?"

It is a fair question, and the honest answer is that the three things cover different ground. Once you see where the edges are, the gap is obvious.

## What ACC covers, and what it does not

ACC covers **accidental injury only**. It pays your treatment costs and compensates 80% of your earnings while you cannot work.

What it does not cover is **illness**. Cancer, heart disease, diabetes, a bad back that came on by itself — none of that is an accident, so none of it is ACC's problem. That single line is where most of the confusion lives. People assume ACC is a general safety net for their health. It is a scheme for injuries.

## What the public system covers, and what you give up

The public system does cover illness, and it is genuinely good at the serious and the urgent. What you give up is control over the timing and over who treats you.

The waits are measurable. Between January and March 2026, 64.9% of people received elective treatment within four months — better than the 57.3% a year earlier, but still more than a third of patients waiting longer ([RNZ, on Health NZ quarterly data](https://www.rnz.co.nz/news/health/617793/more-people-receiving-elective-treatments-sooner-but-ed-wait-times-barely-improve)).

## The three things health insurance actually buys

### 1. Diagnosis and treatment when you need it, not when a queue reaches you

This is the reason everybody thinks of first, and it is real. Months to see a specialist, months again for the scan that confirms what is wrong, potentially a year for surgery. Paying privately out of your own pocket is the alternative, and a gastroscopy alone runs into the thousands.

### 2. Control over your own care

In New Zealand you start at your GP, and your GP decides — based on how serious your case looks — whether you get referred on to a specialist or for further investigation. If your GP judges that you do not meet the referral threshold, your options are to wait and see, or to pay privately.

With health insurance that gate works differently. You can ask for the scan or the specialist appointment, because you are going private and not using public capacity. That is how insurance stops you missing an early diagnosis.

Control also means choosing who treats you. In the public system you see the specialist you are allocated. With insurance you choose.

### 3. The drugs Pharmac does not fund

This is the one people underestimate. Non-funded medicines are the advanced and expensive treatments the government does not subsidise — targeted cancer therapies and immunotherapy among them. Without cover you either pay yourself or go without.

The numbers are not small. Take bowel cancer, and New Zealand has one of the highest rates in the world. Two rounds of a non-funded treatment can run to $50,000, with roughly $1,000 every two weeks after that. Six figures a year is not unusual. With health insurance you get the current best treatment without that being a financial decision.

## Why "I'll buy it when something goes wrong" does not work

If a condition has already been diagnosed — or even if you only have symptoms and no diagnosis yet — it will be excluded from any cover you take out afterwards. Insurance has to be in place while you are well. That is the whole mechanism.

It is also worth knowing that private medical cover in New Zealand generally has **no** general waiting period. Conditions that begin after your policy starts can be claimed, provided you disclosed honestly when you applied. Insurers manage their risk through pre-existing condition exclusions, not by making you wait six months. There are a few specific exceptions — some insurers make you wait a year for wisdom teeth, two to three years for loyalty benefits, one to two years for maternity cover. There is more in our [FAQ](/en/faq/#health-vs-acc).

## Where that leaves you

ACC handles accidents. The public system handles illness on its own timetable. Health insurance buys you the timetable, the choice of specialist, and access to treatments Pharmac does not fund.
